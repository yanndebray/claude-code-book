# Introduction

> "The proper study of man is anything but himself, and it is through the study of language that we learn about our own nature." – J.R.R. Tolkien

Language is a foundation for humanity. It is through language that we have elevated ourselves, from the silence of nature. We shared with one another. We started telling each other stories, sometimes simply relaying information, sometimes making things up. We elaborated ideas, started reasoning about them, turning them into plans. A sound became words — words transformed into writings, writings into books, books into cultures, cultures into civilizations. Leading us all the way to the current digital age, with the inter-connected net of articles, blogs, images, videos. All this content that started to feed machines, eager to learn.

In my previous book, *Programming GPTs*, I wrote about how artificial intelligence had marked a new epoch in our relationship with language — how models built on the crunching of numbers by the largest supercomputers in the world had enabled computers to understand and generate human language with unprecedented accuracy. I described the next wave that was gradually taking the human away from the conversational loop: AI agents. Agents that build on large language models, but that can act autonomously, using tools and carrying out entire workflows without necessarily needing tight supervision.

That book was designed to equip readers with the knowledge to harness the power of agents. This book is something else entirely. This one is personal.

As I was about to respond to a friend who had asked if there was a GPT to look up flight prices to Boston, it got me to reflect on the journey of AI for the past few years. GPTs were a thing — I had written a whole book about them — and then Claude Code arrived. And something shifted. Not just in the technology, but in the relationship. The chatbot I had studied from a distance became a collaborator sitting beside me in the terminal.

This is the story of how I met Claude. But mainly Claude Code.

It was December of 2025. For some reason, this is when the world started to pay attention to Claude Code, and what was happening with coding agents. The company I work for (the MathWorks, developers of MATLAB) was starting a pilot to assess the potential for non-developers first. A few product managers were given access, with the responsibility to report back on their usage in the first quarter of 2026. I was one of the lucky ones.

What I didn't expect was that a three-month pilot would turn into a book. That a coding assistant would become a thinking partner. That I would find myself, night after night, building philosophy websites, competitive intelligence tools, cooking apps, and sports analytics channels — not because anyone asked me to, but because for the first time, the gap between having an idea and making it real had nearly vanished.

This is not a technical manual. It is a memoir of collaboration — an honest account of what happens when a product manager with more curiosity than coding skill meets an AI that doesn't just answer questions, but builds alongside you.
